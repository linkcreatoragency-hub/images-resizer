How to Resize an Image Without Losing Quality

Published August 7, 2026 · Images Resizer Team

Resizing an image sounds simple — and it is — but doing it well requires understanding a few basics. Do it wrong and your photo comes out blurry, pixelated or stretched. Do it right and nobody will ever know the image was resized at all. This guide explains exactly how to resize an image without losing quality, whether you're preparing photos for a website, social media, email or print.

Why images lose quality when resized

A digital image is a grid of pixels. When you resize it, software must create a new grid — either with fewer pixels (downscaling) or more pixels (upscaling) — and calculate the color of every new pixel from the original ones. This calculation is called resampling, and the algorithm used makes all the difference.

Cheap, fast resampling (like "nearest neighbor") simply copies the closest original pixel, which produces jagged edges. High-quality resampling (bicubic, Lanczos, or multi-step area averaging) blends surrounding pixels intelligently, producing smooth, sharp results.

Rule #1: Downscale, don't upscale

Making an image smaller almost always looks great, because the software has more information than it needs. Making an image larger forces the software to invent detail that doesn't exist — which is why enlarged photos look soft or blocky.

Start with the highest-resolution original you have, and resize down to the target size in one step.

Rule #2: Keep the aspect ratio locked

The aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height. If you change one without adjusting the other proportionally, your image will look stretched or squashed. Every good image resizer has a "lock aspect ratio" option — keep it on unless you have a specific reason not to. If you need a different shape (say, square for Instagram), crop the image first, then resize.

Rule #3: Choose the right output format

  • JPG — best for photographs. Use quality 80–90% for a great balance of size and sharpness.
  • PNG — best for screenshots, logos and graphics with text or transparency. Lossless, but larger files.
  • WebP — best for websites. Similar quality to JPG at 25–35% smaller file size.

Not sure which to pick? See our full comparison of JPG vs PNG vs WebP.

Rule #4: Resize for the actual size you need

A common mistake is uploading a 4000-pixel-wide photo to a spot on a web page that displays at 800 pixels. The browser squeezes it down anyway, but visitors pay the price in slow loading. Resize to the real display size (or 2× that size for sharp high-DPI screens) and your pages will load dramatically faster.

Frequently asked questions

What's the best resolution for web images?

For full-width banners, 1920px wide is plenty. For in-content images, 800–1200px wide covers most layouts. Thumbnails rarely need more than 400px.

Can I resize an image bigger without it getting blurry?

Small enlargements (up to ~120%) are usually acceptable. Beyond that, expect softness — no ordinary resizer can create detail that was never captured.

Does resizing remove EXIF data?

Yes — when our tool redraws your image it does not copy EXIF metadata (camera model, GPS location) into the new file, which is actually a privacy benefit.
